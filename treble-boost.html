Treble Boost
Effects Box.

An op-amp guitar pedal that boosts mid/treble frequencies to drive an amp into a biting, distorted tone — hand-designed, SPICE-simulated, breadboarded, and measured on the bench.

HeadlineMeasured peak gain 22.4 dB · ×11 at 1 kHz on the bench · hand calc, SPICE, and measurement compared side by side

Problem

This circuit sits between an electric guitar and its amplifier. It's a non-inverting op-amp gain stage combined with a high-pass and low-pass filter, so it works as a bandpass "treble boost": it attenuates bass, amplifies the 300 Hz–15 kHz range the guitar actually speaks in, and rolls off very high frequencies to keep AM-radio interference out. Driving that boosted signal into the amp's input pushes it into overdrive for a more pleasant distorted tone, with a 10k gain-select pot for how hard it hits.

Approach

Symbolic transfer function

H(ω) ≈ A × HPF × LPF

A  = 1 + (R2 + R4) / R1
HPF = jωR5C3 / (1 + jωR5C3)
LPF = 1 / (1 + jω(R2 + R4)C2)
R5 & C3 set the low cutoff (bass rolloff) | R4 is the 10k gain-select pot | C2 sets the high cutoff (noise/AM rolloff) | C4/C5 (10µF) are DC-blocking coupling caps.

What didn't match, and why

The upper cutoff landed far above the design

Symptom
The hand calculation put the upper cutoff at 14.5 kHz; SPICE gave 24.9 kHz and the bench measured 35 kHz.
Cause
The hand calculation treats each filter as an isolated first-order stage, while the real circuit's stages load each other, and component tolerances shift the corner further.
Takeaway
The first-order model is good for choosing parts, not for predicting the corner. SPICE caught most of the gap before the build.

Measured gain came in below simulation

Symptom
At 1 kHz SPICE predicted a gain of 21; the oscilloscope measured 11. At 100 Hz, 10.5 simulated vs. 3 measured.
Cause
Real components carry tolerance error, and breadboard wiring adds parasitic loading that the ideal model doesn't capture.
Takeaway
The measured response still met the design goal: bass cut (gain 3 at 100 Hz), strong boost in the guitar's band (11 at 1 kHz, 22.4 dB peak), and roll-off above it.

What I learned

  • Compare three ways. Hand math, simulation, and bench measurement each catch different mistakes; putting them in one table made the gaps obvious.
  • Simulate before you solder. SPICE flagged the cutoff shift before the breadboard did.
  • Layout matters at audio frequencies too. Short wire runs and bypass capacitors kept AM-radio interference out of the build.
